# Analysis

BERT (`bert-base-uncased`) has 12 layers x 12 heads. Diagrams for every head are generated by `mask.py`; two are kept in [`diagrams/`](diagrams/). Both were produced from the same input:

> She bought a [MASK] cake for the party.

In each diagram, rows are the tokens doing the attending and columns are the tokens being attended to. Lighter cells mean higher attention.

## Layer 2, Head 7

[`diagrams/Attention_Layer2_Head7.png`](diagrams/Attention_Layer2_Head7.png)

The `[MASK]` column is the brightest in the grid. The tokens `she`, `bought` and `cake` put most of their attention on `[MASK]`, with `bought` the strongest. So in this sentence the head routes information from the words around the masked position into the masked token itself, which is what the model needs in order to predict it. Tokens at the end of the sentence (`party`, `.`, `[SEP]`) instead attend mostly to `[CLS]`.

## Layer 5, Head 2

[`diagrams/Attention_Layer5_Head2.png`](diagrams/Attention_Layer5_Head2.png)

Here most of the attention lands on the `[SEP]` column: `bought`, `cake`, `party` and `[SEP]` all attend to it strongly, and `[CLS]` mostly attends to itself. Heads that default to `[SEP]` are common in BERT, so this one looks like a mostly "resting" head for this input. The few non-`[SEP]` links are weaker: `she` attends to `cake` and `party`, and `for` attends a little to `[MASK]`. In this sentence the head does not show a clear adjective-noun relationship between the masked word and `cake`.

## Example sentences to test further

These were not run through the saved diagrams above, but they are good inputs for looking for a head that tracks coreference or modifier-noun relations:

- The professor entered the room. [MASK] began the lecture right away.
- The cat jumped on the table because [MASK] saw a bird.
- The [MASK] man opened the door.
